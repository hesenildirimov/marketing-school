One assumption comes up again and again in marketing teams: "Our brand is small, but our customers are very loyal." Empirical research shows that, in practice, this statement is almost never true.

## What the law says

Double jeopardy is an observed regularity: a brand with a low market share is **penalised twice**. It has fewer buyers, and those buyers also purchase it less often.

In other words, loyalty is not an independent variable. It is largely a function of market share. Rank the brands in any category by share and then by repeat-purchase rate, and the two rankings track each other.

| Brand share | Number of buyers | Average purchase frequency |
|---|---|---|
| High | Many | High |
| Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Low | Few | Low |

## Why it happens

The reason is not emotional attachment but **availability**. A large brand is present in more stores, on more shelves and at more usage occasions (physical availability), and it is linked to more situations in buyers' memories (mental availability). As a result, it captures a bigger share of everyday, largely habitual purchase moments.

What does a small brand need to do to sell to the same buyer as often? It needs to increase its physical and mental availability — in other words, it needs to grow. Trying to win market share by "fixing" loyalty works the wrong way round.

> Loyalty is an outcome, not a cause. As a brand grows, its loyalty metrics rise automatically.

## What this means in practice

1. **Loyalty programmes should not take the bulk of the budget.** Attempts to push up the purchase frequency of existing customers typically deliver low returns, because frequency is already set by share.
2. **Penetration is the key metric.** Most growth comes from new — and especially light — buyers, rarely from heavy buyers buying even more.
3. **Don't target too narrowly.** Reaching all buyers in the category is more effective than reaching a small segment deeply.
4. **Invest in distinctive brand assets.** Colour, shape, logo, slogan and sound are the memory cues that make a brand easy to recognise, and they are how mental availability works.

## When to be careful

Double jeopardy holds within a category, between comparable brands. There are exceptions: niche brands (limited geography, limited distribution, a premium price tier) can show higher loyalty than expected, because their buyer base is artificially restricted. That does not support the "small but loyal" claim, though — it is a different market structure and cannot be managed with the same strategy.

## The question to ask your team

In your next brand planning discussion, one question clarifies things quickly: **"Does this campaign grow penetration, or does it retarget existing buyers?"** If the answer is the second, someone needs to explain why the budget is there.
